M1 · Chapter 1

The healthcare revenue cycle

It is the path money takes through a medical practice. It starts when a patient is referred for care and ends only when every dollar owed has been collected.

Why it mattersKnowing the cycle shows you how one visit becomes revenue, and where that money can get stuck.

Before the visit get ready

  1. ReferralA physician orders care, or the patient refers themselves.
  2. Intake or pre-registrationThe practice collects demographic and insurance details.

During the visit the heart of it

  1. Patient visit and codingThe provider records diagnosis codes (what is wrong) and procedure codes (what was done). Together they are the birth of the claim.
  2. Code check (sometimes)A medical coder or claim-scrubbing software reviews codes for accuracy and compliance.

After the visit get paid

  1. Billing and claim submissionThe claim goes to the insurance company.
  2. AdjudicationThe insurer accepts the claim and processes it. The result is paid or denied.
  3. Payment posting and reconciliationThe practice records the payment and matches it against the patient's account.
  4. Denial managementDenied claims are tracked, fixed, and worked until payment is recovered.
  5. Working the A/RFollow up on claims that are neither paid nor denied (accounts receivable).
  6. Patient billingAfter the insurer pays, the leftover balance is billed to the patient.
  7. CollectionsIf the balance stays unpaid after statements, the account usually goes to an outside agency that pursues it until paid in full.
Auditing sits on top of everythingAuditing checks every stage to make sure the practice is working efficiently and staying compliant.

Before or after the claim is sent?

Before submission
  • Collect demographic and insurance details
  • Provider records codes
  • Coder or software reviews codes
After submission
  • Insurer adjudicates
  • Practice posts payment and reconciles
Example: James owes $120The insurer paid its share and the statements went unanswered for months. That is patient responsibility, so it goes to collections. It is not a denial, there is nothing to resubmit, and calling the insurer will not help.
M1 · Chapter 2

Copays, coinsurance and deductibles

Three ways a patient shares the cost of care. They sound alike, so here they are side by side.

Copay

  • A flat, round amount, like $20
  • Collected at check-in

Coinsurance

  • A percentage split with the insurer
  • Often an odd number
  • Billed after the claim is adjudicated

Deductible

  • What the patient pays before the insurer starts paying their share

Coinsurance, step by step

An 80/20 split on a visit with a $200 allowed amount:

Insurer $160
You $40
Insurer pays 80%Patient pays 20%

0.20 × $200 = $40

It lands on an odd figure because the percentage is applied to the exact allowed amount. You cannot know it until the claim is adjudicated, which is why it is billed afterward.

M1 · Chapter 3

Out-of-pocket (OOP) maximum

The most a patient will pay for covered care in one plan year. Once they hit it, insurance covers the rest.

What counts toward it
  • Copays
  • Coinsurance
  • Deductibles
What does not count
  • Monthly premiums
  • Services the plan does not cover

Try it: how close is the patient?

Collect carefullyOnce the maximum is met, the patient owes no more copays, coinsurance or deductibles for covered services. Collecting anyway usually leads to refunds and frustrated patients.

Check with the insurer before collecting

  • Do my practice's services count toward the maximum?
  • How close is the patient to it this year?
  • Once it is met, does the patient still owe anything for this service?

Annual reset: the limit starts over each calendar year, or on the plan's own year. A plan with a low limit (around $3,000 to $4,000) can be reached quickly, especially after a surgery.

M1 · Chapter 4

Insurance verification

How a practice confirms with the insurer what a patient's plan will cover for a specific service. Done before the visit, it shows whether the service is covered and what the patient will owe.

Eligibility check

  • Is the person covered?
  • Gives a few basics: deductible, in or out of network, copay for a primary care or specialist visit
  • No service-specific detail

Benefit verification

  • Is this specific service covered?
  • What will it cost the patient?
  • Done by phone or with detailed tools

Your three options

Verification softwareSubscription tools like Availity or TriZetto's gateway. Show coverage for a specialty or service type, whether a deductible applies, and the copay or coinsurance. Usually cannot confirm a specific procedure code, and answers are not always black and white.
Call the insurerUse a standard script written for your specialty and have the procedure codes ready.
Outside vendorA company that calls insurers for you and gathers details like prior authorization requirements.
Tips for calling the insurerReps read answers off their screen, not from deep plan knowledge. Ask short, specific questions, ask them the same way every time, and have the procedure code ready.
ScenarioThe software shows the copay for the service type but not whether the procedure code is covered. Do not assume it is. Call the insurer with your standard script and the code ready.

M2 · Lesson 1

HIPAA and you: patient privacy rights

HIPAA is the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act. It protects health information on paper or on a computer, and it gives patients real rights over it.

Where patients learn their rightsThe Notice of Privacy Practices (NPP) says exactly how information is used, how it is shared, and how the patient's rights are protected.

The patient's six rights

Access
View or get a copy of their records.
Correction
Ask the provider to fix errors in the record.
Statement of disagreement
Add a written statement that stays on file when they disagree.
Accounting of disclosures
Get a report of who the information was shared with.
Confidential contact
Choose which phone number is used and whether messages can be left.
Request limits
Ask that information not be shared with certain people or organizations.

Can the provider share it?

Yes, without permission
  • Coordinating care between doctors on the patient's treatment
  • Reporting flu cases for public health
No, not without consent
  • Sending records to the patient's employer
  • Giving information to anyone who asks
ScenarioAn employer calls and asks about a patient's diagnosis. The patient never gave permission. Decline. Employers have no automatic right to health details. Not even just the diagnosis.
If rights are violatedThe patient can file a complaint with the HHS Office for Civil Rights. Mishandled records bring strict penalties, and the patient must be told right away.
M2 · Lesson 2

HIPAA Security Rule: keeping data secure

Every practice collects patient information, so every practice must keep it secure and confidential. The Security Rule explains how, with a focus on electronic records.

1. Risk analysisStart here to decide what safeguards you need
→
2. Put safeguards in placeAdministrative, physical and technical
→
3. Keep it upSecurity is ongoing, not one and done

Administrative

Office rules and procedures.

  • Who can see which records
  • Staff training

Physical

The building and equipment.

  • Screens and printers away from patients
  • Security locks and alarms

Technical

Hardware and software.

  • Strong passwords and virus protection
  • Audit logs of record changes
  • Encryption of data on hard drives
Why it mattersGood security keeps data safe from unauthorized eyes and accidental leaks, and helps the practice avoid fines and penalties. More tips are on the HHS Office for Civil Rights site, hhs.gov.

M3 · Lesson 1

Introduction to medical billing

After you leave the doctor's office, two steps work hand in hand so the practice gets paid: medical coding, then medical billing.

1. Provider documentsNotes on the visit
→
2. Coder translatesNotes become diagnosis and procedure codes
→
3. Biller sends the claimCodes go to the insurer so money can come in

Medical coding

Comes first. Turns what was done and documented into the billable language of diagnosis codes and procedure codes.

Medical billing

Takes those codes, puts them on the claim, and sends it to the insurer. Think of it as translating services into dollars.

The goal: a zero balance

The patient's journey through the practice is the revenue cycle. It should end back at $0: the insurer owes nothing and the patient owes nothing, because the services were paid for.

Why it matters

Providers get paid
Only for services that were really provided and documented.
Fair processing
Standard codes mean insurers understand claims the same way.
Accurate records
Health and financial records stay consistent.

Two types of billing

Professional billing

  • Physician services and outpatient claims
  • Example: a check-up at a local clinic

Institutional billing

  • Facility costs: hospitals, inpatient clinics, nursing facilities
  • Example: a three-night hospital stay

They use different forms, with different codes and details about what was done and where.

ScenarioA hospital stay after surgery: the facility costs are institutional billing. The surgeons' services would be professional billing.
M3 · Lesson 2

Medical billing vs medical coding

Both live in the revenue cycle, but the work feels different.

Coder: the clinical story

  • Reads documentation, provider notes and pathology reports
  • Assigns standardized code sets
  • Work style: investigative, deep research
  • Often grows into auditing

Biller: the claim's journey

  • Tracks accounts receivable
  • Follows up with insurance payers
  • Manages denials
  • Bills the leftover balance to the patient
  • Work style: interactive problem-solving, calling payers
Easy mix-upsReviewing pathology reports to assign codes is coding. Documenting symptoms and exam notes is the doctor's job. Translating notes into diagnosis codes is the coder's job.

Medical auditing

Reviewing charts proactively to spot documentation gaps and make sure the practice stays compliant. Practices should self-audit regularly instead of waiting for an outside audit. It works like a mirror.

Where coders look when unsure

CMS websiteInsurance policiesCoding Clinic (AHA)CPT Assistant (AMA)Federal Register

Use official sources, not blogs or forums.

ScenarioA new coder is unsure about a guideline and the team is busy. Research the official sources first. If it is still unclear, hold the chart and ask the supervisor. Never guess or skip it.
Not a side gigCoding involves anatomy, medical terminology and complex guidelines, so it cannot be learned over a quick stretch of time.
M3 · Lesson 3

Getting into medical billing and coding

You do not need an expensive degree. Clinics and hospitals often value communication and organization. The trap: employers want experience, but you need a job to get experience. These three tips help you break the loop.

1Take a course

Online or in person, in the style that fits you.

2Get in the door

Apply for any entry-level role at a health organization: scanning, front desk, scheduling. Build relationships, then move into the department you want. Many employers train or even pay for it.

3Find a mentor

Mentorship programs can mean free training and a path to being hired. Example: AAPC with Mentor City. Also market yourself on LinkedIn.

Entry-level roles to aim for

Good starting points
  • Document scanning and records filing
  • Front desk or reception support
  • Patient scheduling and intake
Too advanced for day one
  • Certified coding auditor
  • Revenue cycle director

Pick a course that matches how you learn

Visual learnerVideo demos, chart breakdowns, illustrated workflows
Auditory learnerLecture webinars, discussions, audio walkthroughs
Kinesthetic learnerPractice software, real case simulations, data-entry exercises
ScenarioMaria has no experience, no money for tuition, and wants one-on-one coaching with a possible job. Best fit: a formal mentorship program.
Good to knowWhen specialized roles open up, healthcare systems often hire internally first. Getting in the door early pays off.
M3 · Lesson 4

Why a certified coder matters

A clean claim starts with correct codes. Small clinics sometimes let an office manager or seasoned biller code, but a certified coder protects revenue and compliance.

Proves competence
Knowledge across specialties, not just one office.
Adaptable
A broad foundation, not "how we do it here."
Stays current
Required continuing education units (CEUs), such as 36 to 40 hours every two years.
Lowers risk
Accurate coding tells the patient's real story, so fewer audits and denials.

Two credentials

CPC

Certified Professional Coder, from AAPC.

CCS

Certified Coding Specialist, from AHIMA.

Certification covers organ systems, E/M, pathology, lab, surgery and radiology, plus national coding conventions and legal guidelines. It does not guarantee every claim is approved.

Why repeated codes raise red flags

Diagnoses and modifiers tell the story of what happened, why, and what decisions were made. Insurers run payer analytics software that flags unusual patterns.

Same code: 75%
Other

If a practice bills the same code for about 75% of patients regardless of age, sex or complexity, the account is likely to be flagged for an audit.

Hiring tipIn a hands-on coding test, applicants should be allowed to bring their own official code books and medical dictionaries.

  • AAPC: CPC credential

Needing experience to get hired, but needing a job to get experience.

Which style fits a kinesthetic learner?

Practice software, case simulations and data-entry exercises.

Why can experience alone leave blind spots?

The coder learns one office's habits and may lack the foundation to adapt when guidelines change.

Payer software flags the account for an audit.
