Chapter 1

The healthcare revenue cycle

It is the path money takes through a medical practice. It starts when a patient is referred for care and ends only when every dollar owed has been collected.

Why it mattersKnowing the cycle shows you how one visit becomes revenue, and where that money can get stuck.

Before the visit get ready

  1. ReferralA physician orders care, or the patient refers themselves.
  2. Intake or pre-registrationThe practice collects demographic and insurance details.

During the visit the heart of it

  1. Patient visit and codingThe provider records diagnosis codes (what is wrong) and procedure codes (what was done). Together they are the birth of the claim.
  2. Code check (sometimes)A medical coder or claim-scrubbing software reviews codes for accuracy and compliance.

After the visit get paid

  1. Billing and claim submissionThe claim goes to the insurance company.
  2. AdjudicationThe insurer accepts the claim and processes it. The result is paid or denied.
  3. Payment posting and reconciliationThe practice records the payment and matches it against the patient's account.
  4. Denial managementDenied claims are tracked, fixed, and worked until payment is recovered.
  5. Working the A/RFollow up on claims that are neither paid nor denied (accounts receivable).
  6. Patient billingAfter the insurer pays, the leftover balance is billed to the patient.
  7. CollectionsIf the balance stays unpaid after statements, the account usually goes to an outside agency that pursues it until paid in full.
Auditing sits on top of everythingAuditing checks every stage to make sure the practice is working efficiently and staying compliant.

Before or after the claim is sent?

Before submission
  • Collect demographic and insurance details
  • Provider records codes
  • Coder or software reviews codes
After submission
  • Insurer adjudicates
  • Practice posts payment and reconciles
Example: James owes $120The insurer paid its share and the statements went unanswered for months. That is patient responsibility, so it goes to collections. It is not a denial, there is nothing to resubmit, and calling the insurer will not help.
Chapter 2

Copays, coinsurance and deductibles

Three ways a patient shares the cost of care. They sound alike, so here they are side by side.

Copay

  • A flat, round amount, like $20
  • Collected at check-in

Coinsurance

  • A percentage split with the insurer
  • Often an odd number
  • Billed after the claim is adjudicated

Deductible

  • What the patient pays before the insurer starts paying their share

Coinsurance, step by step

An 80/20 split on a visit with a $200 allowed amount:

Insurer $160
You $40
Insurer pays 80%Patient pays 20%

0.20 × $200 = $40

It lands on an odd figure because the percentage is applied to the exact allowed amount. You cannot know it until the claim is adjudicated, which is why it is billed afterward.

Chapter 3

Out-of-pocket (OOP) maximum

The most a patient will pay for covered care in one plan year. Once they hit it, insurance covers the rest.

What counts toward it
  • Copays
  • Coinsurance
  • Deductibles
What does not count
  • Monthly premiums
  • Services the plan does not cover

Try it: how close is the patient?

Collect carefullyOnce the maximum is met, the patient owes no more copays, coinsurance or deductibles for covered services. Collecting anyway usually leads to refunds and frustrated patients.

Check with the insurer before collecting

  • Do my practice's services count toward the maximum?
  • How close is the patient to it this year?
  • Once it is met, does the patient still owe anything for this service?

Annual reset: the limit starts over each calendar year, or on the plan's own year. A plan with a low limit (around $3,000 to $4,000) can be reached quickly, especially after a surgery.

Chapter 4

Insurance verification

How a practice confirms with the insurer what a patient's plan will cover for a specific service. Done before the visit, it shows whether the service is covered and what the patient will owe.

Eligibility check

  • Is the person covered?
  • Gives a few basics: deductible, in or out of network, copay for a primary care or specialist visit
  • No service-specific detail

Benefit verification

  • Is this specific service covered?
  • What will it cost the patient?
  • Done by phone or with detailed tools

Your three options

Verification softwareSubscription tools like Availity or TriZetto's gateway. Show coverage for a specialty or service type, whether a deductible applies, and the copay or coinsurance. Usually cannot confirm a specific procedure code, and answers are not always black and white.
Call the insurerUse a standard script written for your specialty and have the procedure codes ready.
Outside vendorA company that calls insurers for you and gathers details like prior authorization requirements.
Tips for calling the insurerReps read answers off their screen, not from deep plan knowledge. Ask short, specific questions, ask them the same way every time, and have the procedure code ready.
ScenarioThe software shows the copay for the service type but not whether the procedure code is covered. Do not assume it is. Call the insurer with your standard script and the code ready.
