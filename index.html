M1 · Chapter 1

The healthcare revenue cycle

It is the path money takes through a medical practice. It starts when a patient is referred for care and ends only when every dollar owed has been collected.

Why it mattersKnowing the cycle shows you how one visit becomes revenue, and where that money can get stuck.

Before the visit get ready

  1. ReferralA physician orders care, or the patient refers themselves.
  2. Intake or pre-registrationThe practice collects demographic and insurance details.

During the visit the heart of it

  1. Patient visit and codingThe provider records diagnosis codes (what is wrong) and procedure codes (what was done). Together they are the birth of the claim.
  2. Code check (sometimes)A medical coder or claim-scrubbing software reviews codes for accuracy and compliance.

After the visit get paid

  1. Billing and claim submissionThe claim goes to the insurance company.
  2. AdjudicationThe insurer accepts the claim and processes it. The result is paid or denied.
  3. Payment posting and reconciliationThe practice records the payment and matches it against the patient's account.
  4. Denial managementDenied claims are tracked, fixed, and worked until payment is recovered.
  5. Working the A/RFollow up on claims that are neither paid nor denied (accounts receivable).
  6. Patient billingAfter the insurer pays, the leftover balance is billed to the patient.
  7. CollectionsIf the balance stays unpaid after statements, the account usually goes to an outside agency that pursues it until paid in full.
Auditing sits on top of everythingAuditing checks every stage to make sure the practice is working efficiently and staying compliant.

Before or after the claim is sent?

Before submission
  • Collect demographic and insurance details
  • Provider records codes
  • Coder or software reviews codes
After submission
  • Insurer adjudicates
  • Practice posts payment and reconciles
Example: James owes $120The insurer paid its share and the statements went unanswered for months. That is patient responsibility, so it goes to collections. It is not a denial, there is nothing to resubmit, and calling the insurer will not help.
M1 · Chapter 2

Copays, coinsurance and deductibles

Three ways a patient shares the cost of care. They sound alike, so here they are side by side.

Copay

  • A flat, round amount, like $20
  • Collected at check-in

Coinsurance

  • A percentage split with the insurer
  • Often an odd number
  • Billed after the claim is adjudicated

Deductible

  • What the patient pays before the insurer starts paying their share

Coinsurance, step by step

An 80/20 split on a visit with a $200 allowed amount:

Insurer $160
You $40
Insurer pays 80%Patient pays 20%

0.20 × $200 = $40

It lands on an odd figure because the percentage is applied to the exact allowed amount. You cannot know it until the claim is adjudicated, which is why it is billed afterward.

M1 · Chapter 3

Out-of-pocket (OOP) maximum

The most a patient will pay for covered care in one plan year. Once they hit it, insurance covers the rest.

What counts toward it
  • Copays
  • Coinsurance
  • Deductibles
What does not count
  • Monthly premiums
  • Services the plan does not cover

Try it: how close is the patient?

Collect carefullyOnce the maximum is met, the patient owes no more copays, coinsurance or deductibles for covered services. Collecting anyway usually leads to refunds and frustrated patients.

Check with the insurer before collecting

  • Do my practice's services count toward the maximum?
  • How close is the patient to it this year?
  • Once it is met, does the patient still owe anything for this service?

Annual reset: the limit starts over each calendar year, or on the plan's own year. A plan with a low limit (around $3,000 to $4,000) can be reached quickly, especially after a surgery.

M1 · Chapter 4

Insurance verification

How a practice confirms with the insurer what a patient's plan will cover for a specific service. Done before the visit, it shows whether the service is covered and what the patient will owe.

Eligibility check

  • Is the person covered?
  • Gives a few basics: deductible, in or out of network, copay for a primary care or specialist visit
  • No service-specific detail

Benefit verification

  • Is this specific service covered?
  • What will it cost the patient?
  • Done by phone or with detailed tools

Your three options

Verification softwareSubscription tools like Availity or TriZetto's gateway. Show coverage for a specialty or service type, whether a deductible applies, and the copay or coinsurance. Usually cannot confirm a specific procedure code, and answers are not always black and white.
Call the insurerUse a standard script written for your specialty and have the procedure codes ready.
Outside vendorA company that calls insurers for you and gathers details like prior authorization requirements.
Tips for calling the insurerReps read answers off their screen, not from deep plan knowledge. Ask short, specific questions, ask them the same way every time, and have the procedure code ready.
ScenarioThe software shows the copay for the service type but not whether the procedure code is covered. Do not assume it is. Call the insurer with your standard script and the code ready.

M2 · Lesson 1

HIPAA and you: patient privacy rights

HIPAA is the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act. It protects health information on paper or on a computer, and it gives patients real rights over it.

Where patients learn their rightsThe Notice of Privacy Practices (NPP) says exactly how information is used, how it is shared, and how the patient's rights are protected.

The patient's six rights

Access
View or get a copy of their records.
Correction
Ask the provider to fix errors in the record.
Statement of disagreement
Add a written statement that stays on file when they disagree.
Accounting of disclosures
Get a report of who the information was shared with.
Confidential contact
Choose which phone number is used and whether messages can be left.
Request limits
Ask that information not be shared with certain people or organizations.

Can the provider share it?

Yes, without permission
  • Coordinating care between doctors on the patient's treatment
  • Reporting flu cases for public health
No, not without consent
  • Sending records to the patient's employer
  • Giving information to anyone who asks
ScenarioAn employer calls and asks about a patient's diagnosis. The patient never gave permission. Decline. Employers have no automatic right to health details. Not even just the diagnosis.
If rights are violatedThe patient can file a complaint with the HHS Office for Civil Rights. Mishandled records bring strict penalties, and the patient must be told right away.
M2 · Lesson 2

HIPAA Security Rule: keeping data secure

Every practice collects patient information, so every practice must keep it secure and confidential. The Security Rule explains how, with a focus on electronic records.

1. Risk analysisStart here to decide what safeguards you need
→
2. Put safeguards in placeAdministrative, physical and technical
→
3. Keep it upSecurity is ongoing, not one and done

Administrative

Office rules and procedures.

  • Who can see which records
  • Staff training

Physical

The building and equipment.

  • Screens and printers away from patients
  • Security locks and alarms

Technical

Hardware and software.

  • Strong passwords and virus protection
  • Audit logs of record changes
  • Encryption of data on hard drives
Why it mattersGood security keeps data safe from unauthorized eyes and accidental leaks, and helps the practice avoid fines and penalties. More tips are on the HHS Office for Civil Rights site, hhs.gov.

M3 · Lesson 1

Introduction to medical billing

After you leave the doctor's office, two steps work hand in hand so the practice gets paid: medical coding, then medical billing.

1. Provider documentsNotes on the visit
→
2. Coder translatesNotes become diagnosis and procedure codes
→
3. Biller sends the claimCodes go to the insurer so money can come in

Medical coding

Comes first. Turns what was done and documented into the billable language of diagnosis codes and procedure codes.

Medical billing

Takes those codes, puts them on the claim, and sends it to the insurer. Think of it as translating services into dollars.

The goal: a zero balance

The patient's journey through the practice is the revenue cycle. It should end back at $0: the insurer owes nothing and the patient owes nothing, because the services were paid for.

Why it matters

Providers get paid
Only for services that were really provided and documented.
Fair processing
Standard codes mean insurers understand claims the same way.
Accurate records
Health and financial records stay consistent.

Two types of billing

Professional billing

  • Physician services and outpatient claims
  • Example: a check-up at a local clinic

Institutional billing

  • Facility costs: hospitals, inpatient clinics, nursing facilities
  • Example: a three-night hospital stay

They use different forms, with different codes and details about what was done and where.

ScenarioA hospital stay after surgery: the facility costs are institutional billing. The surgeons' services would be professional billing.
M3 · Lesson 2

Medical billing vs medical coding

Both live in the revenue cycle, but the work feels different.

Coder: the clinical story

  • Reads documentation, provider notes and pathology reports
  • Assigns standardized code sets
  • Work style: investigative, deep research
  • Often grows into auditing

Biller: the claim's journey

  • Tracks accounts receivable
  • Follows up with insurance payers
  • Manages denials
  • Bills the leftover balance to the patient
  • Work style: interactive problem-solving, calling payers
Easy mix-upsReviewing pathology reports to assign codes is coding. Documenting symptoms and exam notes is the doctor's job. Translating notes into diagnosis codes is the coder's job.

Medical auditing

Reviewing charts proactively to spot documentation gaps and make sure the practice stays compliant. Practices should self-audit regularly instead of waiting for an outside audit. It works like a mirror.

Where coders look when unsure

CMS websiteInsurance policiesCoding Clinic (AHA)CPT Assistant (AMA)Federal Register

Use official sources, not blogs or forums.

ScenarioA new coder is unsure about a guideline and the team is busy. Research the official sources first. If it is still unclear, hold the chart and ask the supervisor. Never guess or skip it.
Not a side gigCoding involves anatomy, medical terminology and complex guidelines, so it cannot be learned over a quick stretch of time.
M3 · Lesson 3

Getting into medical billing and coding

You do not need an expensive degree. Clinics and hospitals often value communication and organization. The trap: employers want experience, but you need a job to get experience. These three tips help you break the loop.

1Take a course

Online or in person, in the style that fits you.

2Get in the door

Apply for any entry-level role at a health organization: scanning, front desk, scheduling. Build relationships, then move into the department you want. Many employers train or even pay for it.

3Find a mentor

Mentorship programs can mean free training and a path to being hired. Example: AAPC with Mentor City. Also market yourself on LinkedIn.

Entry-level roles to aim for

Good starting points
  • Document scanning and records filing
  • Front desk or reception support
  • Patient scheduling and intake
Too advanced for day one
  • Certified coding auditor
  • Revenue cycle director

Pick a course that matches how you learn

Visual learnerVideo demos, chart breakdowns, illustrated workflows
Auditory learnerLecture webinars, discussions, audio walkthroughs
Kinesthetic learnerPractice software, real case simulations, data-entry exercises
ScenarioMaria has no experience, no money for tuition, and wants one-on-one coaching with a possible job. Best fit: a formal mentorship program.
Good to knowWhen specialized roles open up, healthcare systems often hire internally first. Getting in the door early pays off.
M3 · Lesson 4

Why a certified coder matters

A clean claim starts with correct codes. Small clinics sometimes let an office manager or seasoned biller code, but a certified coder protects revenue and compliance.

Proves competence
Knowledge across specialties, not just one office.
Adaptable
A broad foundation, not "how we do it here."
Stays current
Required continuing education units (CEUs), such as 36 to 40 hours every two years.
Lowers risk
Accurate coding tells the patient's real story, so fewer audits and denials.

Two credentials

CPC

Certified Professional Coder, from AAPC.

CCS

Certified Coding Specialist, from AHIMA.

Certification covers organ systems, E/M, pathology, lab, surgery and radiology, plus national coding conventions and legal guidelines. It does not guarantee every claim is approved.

Why repeated codes raise red flags

Diagnoses and modifiers tell the story of what happened, why, and what decisions were made. Insurers run payer analytics software that flags unusual patterns.

Same code: 75%
Other

If a practice bills the same code for about 75% of patients regardless of age, sex or complexity, the account is likely to be flagged for an audit.

Hiring tipIn a hands-on coding test, applicants should be allowed to bring their own official code books and medical dictionaries.

M4 · Topic 1

The code sets

A claim tells the payer two things: why the patient was seen, and what was done. Each answer has its own code set.

ICD-10-CM: the "why"

Diagnoses, symptoms and conditions. Letter first, then numbers.

J02.9 Acute pharyngitis, unspecified

CPT: the "what"

Procedures and services a provider performs, including office visits. Five digits.

99213 Established patient office visit

HCPCS Level II: the "things"

Supplies, equipment, ambulance rides and some drugs. A letter plus four digits.

E0110 Crutches

Modifiers: the "details"

Two characters added to a CPT or HCPCS code to change how it is read.

-25 separate visit, same day as a procedure
-LT / -RT left or right side

How the codes fit together on one visit

DiagnosisJ02.9 sore throat
→
Visit99213 office visit
→
Test87880 rapid strep test
Medical necessityEvery procedure on the claim needs a diagnosis that explains why it was done. A strep test with no throat complaint on the claim is a likely denial.

M3 and M4 wrap-up

The highlights the course listed at the end of the pre-work, then a quick check.

  • Coders turn the doctor's notes into diagnosis and procedure codes. Billers turn those codes into claims and payment.
  • Professional billing covers physician and outpatient services. Institutional billing covers facility costs.

  • Most employers look for AAPC or AHIMA credentials.
  • Pick a course that fits how you learn, and beat the experience trap with an entry-level role or a mentor.

M5 · Topic 1

What does a medical biller do?

The course puts the whole job in four verbs. Every claim is a request for payment from someone other than the patient, and the biller sees it through to the end.

1Check

Patient details, insurance, codes and charges, before anything goes out.

2Send

Submit the claim electronically, usually through a clearinghouse, to the right payer.

3Follow up

Check claim status in portals, call payers, and work the A/R list.

4Resolve

Post payments, bill the patient's share, fix rejections and appeal unfair denials.
The heart of the jobFollow-up. A claim that is sent and never tracked is money the practice may never see.
M5 · Topic 2

Understanding insurances

Insurance falls into two families: government and commercial. The line blurs when a private company runs a government plan.

Government

  • Medicare: people 65+, some people with disabilities, kidney failure. Federal, run by CMS. Claims go to regional contractors called MACs.
  • Medicaid: low income, run by each state. Usually pays last.

Blended

  • Medicare Advantage: a private plan that manages a patient's Medicare
  • Medicaid HMO: a private plan that manages a patient's Medicaid
  • Bill the plan on the card, not the government program

Commercial

  • Private insurers, such as Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan (BCBSM)
  • Employer and individual plans
  • Each has its own rules, portal and filing limit

Reading an insurance card

  • Member ID exactly as printed, including the prefix. On a BCBS card, the first three letters route the claim to the right Blue plan.
  • Group number for employer plans
  • Plan type and network: HMO, PPO or Medicare Advantage
  • Copay amounts, payer ID or claims address
  • Phone numbers for provider services and prior authorization
Always copy both sidesThe front shows who the patient is. The back shows where the claim goes and who to call.
ScenarioA 70-year-old shows a card from a private insurer marked "Medicare Advantage." Bill that plan, not original Medicare.

M5 · Topic 5

Verifying patient insurance eligibility

Many revenue cycle problems start before a claim exists. Check coverage before the visit, ideally online.

Eligibility check

  • Is coverage active on the date of service?
  • Is this the right plan and member?

Benefit verification

  • What does the plan cover for this service?
  • Deductible, copay, coinsurance
  • Referral or prior authorization needed?
  • Is the provider in network?

Five ways to check coverage

1. Payer portalThe insurer's own website
2. Multi-payer portalAvaility and similar, one login for many payers
3. Real-time checkFrom the practice software or clearinghouse (the electronic 270/271 request and response)
4. Phone menu (IVR)The payer's automated phone line
5. Payer representativeA live call, best for service-specific questions
An estimate, not a guaranteeThe payer makes the final decision when it processes the claim. Write down who you spoke to, the call reference number and the date.
M5 · Topic 6

Building a clean claim every time

A clean claim is one the payer accepts and pays the first time, with no corrections. It has four ingredients.

1Verified data

Name, date of birth, member ID and address match the payer's records.

2Matched codes

Each procedure is backed by a diagnosis that explains it, with the right modifiers.

3Payer requirements met

Prior authorization number, referral, provider NPI, whatever that payer asks for.

4On-time submission

Sent inside the payer's timely filing limit.
M5 · Topic 7

Claims adjudication

How the insurer decides whether to pay, and how much. Four steps.

  1. 1. Initial processing reviewBasic checks for missing or invalid data. Claims that fail here can be rejected right away.
  2. 2. Automatic reviewSoftware checks coverage, code edits, duplicates and medical necessity rules.
  3. 3. Manual reviewA claims examiner looks at flagged claims and may ask for medical records. This is where delays begin.
  4. 4. Payment determinationPaid, denied or reduced. The result comes back on an EOB or ERA (see M6).

M6 · Medical Billing, Part 2

A claim has been sent. Now what comes back, and what to do with it.

M6 · Topic 1

Reading the EOB and ERA

After adjudication the payer explains its decision line by line. Reading it right is the difference between billing the patient and filing a pointless appeal.

EOB

Explanation of Benefits. A paper or PDF statement. Patients get one too.

ERA

Electronic Remittance Advice (the 835 file). Practice software can use it to post payments automatically.

The lines to read

Billed amountWhat the practice charged
Allowed amountThe most the payer recognizes under the contract
Contractual adjustmentBilled minus allowed. The practice writes it off.
Patient responsibilityDeductible, coinsurance and copay. Goes on the patient's bill.
Paid amountWhat the payer actually paid
Adjustment and remark codesWhy the numbers came out this way (CARC and RARC codes)

Group codes decide who owes what

CO

Contractual obligation. The provider writes it off and cannot bill the patient.

PR

Patient responsibility. PR-1 deductible, PR-2 coinsurance, PR-3 copay.

OA and PI

Other adjustment, and payer-initiated adjustment. Less common.

Worked example: a $0 payment

Billed $200. Allowed $120. Paid $0. Adjustments: CO-45 $80 and PR-1 $120.

Write-off $80
Patient $120
CO-45: over the fee schedulePR-1: deductible

The claim was approved. The allowed amount went toward the patient's unmet deductible, so the practice bills the patient $120.

$0 + PR-1 is not a denialIt is the deductible. Bill the patient. Do not appeal.
M6 · Topic 2

Rejected vs denied claims

Both mean "not paid," but they happen at different points and need different responses.

Rejected

  • Never reached the payer's system. It failed at the clearinghouse or the payer's front-door checks.
  • Causes: invalid member ID, wrong birth date, missing NPI, format errors
  • Shows on clearinghouse reports, not the ERA
  • Fix it and resubmit fast. It counts as never filed, so the filing clock keeps running.

Denied

  • Processed, and the payer decided not to pay
  • Causes: not covered, no prior authorization, not medically necessary, filed late, duplicate
  • Shows on the ERA with a reason code
  • Investigate before you act. Then correct and resubmit, appeal, or bill the patient if the plan allows.
M6 · Topic 3

Why claims don't get paid

Many errors start long before the claim is created: at the front desk.

~1 in 4

claim errors start at the front desk: a misspelled name, a wrong birth date, an old card, the wrong primary payer, a missing referral.

Three habits that prevent them

1Verify eligibility

Before every visit, not just the first one.

2Keep data clean

Copy the card exactly and confirm details at each visit.

3Use a checklist

Every registration captures the same required items.
M6 · Topic 4

What is timely filing?

The deadline for the payer to receive a claim, counted from the date of service. Miss it and the claim is denied, and the patient usually cannot be billed for it either.

Every payer is different
Medicare allows 12 months from the date of service. Commercial and Medicaid limits range from about 90 days to a year.
Trust the contract
The provider agreement is the only reliable source. Cheat sheets and old websites go out of date.
Keep proofSave clearinghouse acceptance reports. They prove the claim was sent on time if a payer says it was late.
M6 · Topic 5

What is A/R in healthcare?

While a claim waits, the practice has not been paid for care it already gave. That unpaid money is accounts receivable.

A/R

How much the practice is owed.

Aged receivables

How long it has been owed, sorted into day buckets.

Aging buckets

0 to 30 daysNormal. Most clean claims pay here.
31 to 60 daysStart following up on claim status.
61 to 90 daysCall the payer, find the problem, fix it.
91 to 120 daysUrgent. Filing and appeal deadlines get close.
120+ daysLeast likely to be paid. Work the biggest balances first.
M6 · Topic 6

Patient registration

Much of what gets stuck in A/R started with a problem at check-in. Good registration feeds a clean claim from the start.

  1. DemographicsFull legal name, date of birth, address, phone
  2. Cards and IDScan both sides of every insurance card, plus a photo ID
  3. PolicyholderWho holds the policy and how the patient is related to them
  4. Order of payersPrimary, secondary (coordination of benefits)
  5. Eligibility and authorizationCoverage, benefits, referral, prior authorization
  6. Signed formsHIPAA acknowledgment, financial policy, assignment of benefits
Assignment of benefits (AOB)The patient's signed permission for the insurer to pay the provider directly. Without it, the payer may send the check to the patient.

M7 · Topic 1

Using AI honestly and responsibly

Used well, AI takes over busy work so you can focus on judgment calls. Used carelessly, it spreads errors and exposes private information. Three principles guide every use.

Truthfulness

Never pass off unchecked AI output as fact, and never put invented facts in a claim or appeal.

Integrity

Do your own thinking first, then use AI as a partner to critique and refine it.

Accountability

You own what you send, even if AI drafted it.

Keep private data outPersonal, patient, financial and company information stays out of AI tools unless your employer's policy allows it. When in doubt, leave it out.
Better promptsClear, specific prompts with context get better results.
M7 · Topic 2

The five kinds of AI

AI will not replace billers. It takes over repetitive work (eligibility checks, claim scrubbing, status checks, appeal drafts) while the biller stays the decision-maker.

Machine learningLearns patterns from past data. Spots claims likely to be denied.
Natural language processingReads human language. Reads provider notes and suggests codes.
Predictive AIForecasts what comes next. Predicts payment timing and denial risk.
AutomationRuns rule-based, repetitive steps. Eligibility checks, status checks, payment posting.
Generative AICreates new text. Drafts appeal letters and patient messages.

M8 · Billing and Coding Practice with AI Tools

Hands-on practice across the whole cycle. No scoring: catch the AI's mistakes and decide when to trust it.

M8 · Practice

The four practice exercises

Each follows the revenue cycle, from the front desk to the analytics dashboard. All patients and amounts are fictional.

1The front end

Catch a coverage gap the AI eligibility tool missed, compare an AI-filled registration with the scanned card, write a prompt with no patient details, handle a prior authorization flag.

"Ready" is not proof. Check the card.

2Coding with an AI assistant

Review suggested codes for a sore throat, an ankle injury and a diabetes follow-up. Remove unsupported codes and write a non-leading provider query.

The AI suggests codes. The note decides.

3Claims, denials and appeals

Sort scrubber flags into real errors and false alarms, judge a modifier auto-fix, order denials by appeal deadline, check an AI appeal for invented facts.

Fast is only good if it's right.

4Posting, analytics and workflow

Check an auto-posted payment against the remittance, separate facts from predictions on a dashboard, pick a first AI pilot.

Check the numbers, then decide where AI goes next.

M8 · Key idea

The three kinds of AI mistakes

Know these by name. Every exercise asks you to spot one.

Hallucination

Something that is not in the record: an invented code, fact or policy citation.

False negative

Something the AI missed: a documented procedure left uncoded, a coverage gap not flagged.

False positive

A problem that is not real: a "duplicate claim" that does not exist.

Asking the provider the right way

Leading (avoid)

"Please confirm the patient has uncontrolled diabetes with hyperglycemia."

Non-leading (use)

"Glucose of 280 is documented. Please clarify the diabetes status: controlled, with hyperglycemia, other, or unable to determine."

The ruleGive the clinical facts and neutral options. Never steer toward the answer that pays more.
M8 · Wrap-up

Five rules for working with AI

1Check against the source

The card, the note, the remittance. A "high confidence" label does not make it correct.

2Watch for the three mistakes

Hallucinations, false negatives, false positives.

3Documentation decides

Every code needs a sentence in the note. CO and PR codes decide who owes what.

4Protect patient information

Use placeholders, approved tools only, and check every draft before it goes out.

5Fix the cause, then let results guide AI

Expand a tool only when it saves time, improves accuracy, cuts denials and earns staff trust.
